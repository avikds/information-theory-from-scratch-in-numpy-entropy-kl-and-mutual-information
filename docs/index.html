Information Theory from Scratch in NumPy: Entropy, KL and Mutual Information

How it was built

Part 1: Bits, Codes and Names

Entropy, cross entropy and perplexity (sections 6.1.1, 6.1.2 and 6.1.5) measured on something real: 32,033 first names. You write the formulas, build Huffman codes so that a bit is an actual bit, fit n-gram language models, read perplexity as a branching factor, and compress held-out names with your own codes.

Part 2: Joint Tables and the Chain Rule

Joint entropy, conditional entropy and mutual information from a table (sections 6.1.3, 6.1.4, 6.3.2 and 6.3.3), starting with the book's even and prime numbers and Exercise 6.3, then applied to pairs and triples of letters in names, including the case where seeing an observation makes you less certain.

Part 3: Sequence Logos and the Price of Estimating Entropy

The DNA application of section 6.1.1.1: position weight matrices, information per column and the consensus sequence, on an aligned motif whose true distribution you know. Then section 6.1.1.2's warning made numeric: plug-in entropy runs low, a fixed correction helps with few states, and a long tail defeats both.

Part 4: Relative Entropy: KL, Gaussians, Forward and Reverse

KL divergence as extra bits (section 6.2), differential entropy read through quantization (6.1.6), the Gaussian formula (6.2.3), maximum likelihood as KL minimization (6.2.5), and the two directions of KL (6.2.6): a one-Gaussian fit to a bimodal target that covers both modes or locks onto one, followed by the three minima of Exercise 6.4.

Part 5: Estimating Mutual Information

Mutual information as a generalized correlation coefficient (section 6.3.5): the Gaussian closed form, the histogram plug-in with Scott's rule (6.1.6.3) and its sensitivity to bins, and the KSG k-nearest-neighbour estimator, finishing with a showdown on Gaussian, warped and nonlinear data whose true mutual information is known.

Part 6: A Normalized Measure: MIC

Normalized mutual information on continuous data is sensitive to quantization (section 6.3.6), so the maximal information coefficient (6.3.7) searches over grids. You write the dynamic program that optimizes grid boundaries along one axis, build the characteristic matrix, and rerun Figure 6.6, checking the equitability claim along the way.

Part 7: Data Processing and Sufficient Statistics

Conditional mutual information on a noisy channel chain X -> Y -> Z (sections 6.3.4 and 6.3.8): processing cannot create information about the source, though estimates can appear to. Then sufficient statistics (6.3.9) measured exactly, by enumerating every sequence of Bernoulli trials.

Part 8: Feature Selection and Fano's Bound

The chapter's closing argument (section 6.3.10): features with high mutual information are worth keeping because Fano's inequality links H(Y|X) to the best possible error. You rank tumour features, find that a top-ranked list can be one measurement three times over, compute both forms of the bound, and compare them with real test errors on digits.
